## Why subtypes exist

POTS is a syndrome, not a single disease mechanism. The same diagnostic pattern can arise from different problems in circulation, nerve signaling, blood volume, immune function, hormones, connective tissue, or physical conditioning after illness. Subtypes help clinicians describe dominant patterns. They do not divide patients into clean categories.

A patient may show features of neuropathic POTS, hyperadrenergic POTS, and hypovolemic POTS at the same time. Another patient may shift patterns across time. Subtype language helps organize thinking, but it should not become a rigid identity.

### Neuropathic POTS

Neuropathic POTS refers to impaired nerve control of blood vessels, especially in the lower body. When a healthy person stands, nerves signal blood vessels in the legs and abdomen to constrict. This helps push blood back toward the heart and brain. If those signals are weak or patchy, blood pools downward. The heart then speeds up to compensate.

People with neuropathic features may notice worse symptoms after standing still, warm showers, hot weather, or large meals. They may have dependent color changes in the legs, such as redness, purple discoloration, or mottling while upright. Some also have abnormal sweating or signs of small fiber neuropathy, though not every patient receives specialized nerve testing.

### Hyperadrenergic POTS

Hyperadrenergic POTS describes a pattern with excessive sympathetic nervous system activation while upright. “Adrenergic” refers to adrenaline-like signaling. Patients may experience a pounding heart, tremor, anxiety-like body sensations, sweating, cold extremities, chest discomfort, and a sense of internal vibration. Blood pressure may rise on standing in some patients.

This pattern can feel alarming because the body behaves as if it is under threat even when the person is simply standing. The symptoms are physical. They can resemble panic, but the trigger is often posture, exertion, heat, or other autonomic stress.

#### Hypovolemic POTS

Hypovolemic POTS refers to low circulating blood volume. “Hypo” means low, and “volemic” refers to volume. If the body has too little effective circulating volume, standing becomes harder because there is less blood available to return to the heart.

Patients with hypovolemic features may feel worse with dehydration, sweating, heat, diarrhea, inadequate intake, or illness. Treatment discussions often focus on fluids, salt when medically appropriate, and medications that support volume or vascular tone. These choices require clinical guidance, especially for people with kidney disease, hypertension, heart disease, pregnancy, or other medical constraints.

#### Secondary POTS

Secondary POTS means the POTS pattern appears in association with another condition. Examples include autoimmune disorders, diabetes, infections, long COVID, hypermobility-related conditions, mast cell activation symptoms, and other illnesses that affect nerves, blood vessels, inflammation, or conditioning.

Secondary does not mean less real. It means clinicians should look for a driver that also needs treatment. A person can meet POTS criteria and still need evaluation for anemia, thyroid disease, autoimmune disease, neuropathy, or medication effects.

## Overlap is common

The most important subtype lesson is overlap. A patient with partial nerve dysfunction may also have low blood volume. Low blood volume can trigger stronger sympathetic activation. Hypermobile connective tissue may worsen venous pooling. Deconditioning after months of symptoms can intensify tachycardia. The body works as one system, so labels often blur.

This is why treatment is usually layered. A clinician may start with education, hydration, salt if appropriate, compression, and graded exercise. Then they may adjust medications based on the dominant pattern: heart rate control, blood vessel constriction, blood volume support, or sympathetic dampening.

### Why subtype testing is limited

No single test assigns a perfect subtype. Standing vitals, tilt-table testing, norepinephrine levels, autonomic reflex testing, sweat testing, blood volume assessment, skin biopsy, and clinical history all provide clues. Many clinics do not offer every test. Even when testing exists, results may not map neatly to treatment.

A practical approach asks: What worsens symptoms? What improves symptoms? Does blood pressure rise, fall, or stay stable? Are there neuropathy signs? Is blood volume likely low? Are there immune, connective tissue, or mast cell clues? Which treatment target best fits the patient’s pattern and risks?

### Treatment follows physiology

Subtype thinking helps match treatment to physiology. A patient with prominent venous pooling may benefit from compression and medications that support vascular tone. A patient with hypovolemic features may focus on volume expansion strategies. A patient with hyperadrenergic features may need careful reduction of sympathetic overactivity. A patient with secondary POTS needs attention to the associated condition.

Treatment still starts with basics because the basics support multiple patterns. Fluids, salt when safe, compression, pacing, sleep support, and structured recumbent exercise often help across subtypes.

#### The role of hypermobility

Hypermobility can contribute to orthostatic symptoms. More flexible connective tissue may allow veins to stretch more, which can increase blood pooling. Joint pain, instability, fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms, and headache may complicate daily management. Not every person with POTS is hypermobile, and not every hypermobile person has POTS, but the overlap matters.

#### The role of long COVID and infection

Some patients develop POTS after viral illness, including COVID-19. Post-infectious POTS may involve immune activation, autonomic nerve dysfunction, vascular changes, deconditioning after illness, or multiple mechanisms. Long COVID also includes symptoms that overlap with POTS, such as fatigue, brain fog, palpitations, shortness of breath, and exercise intolerance.

#### The role of mast cell symptoms

Some POTS patients report flushing, hives, itching, swelling, wheezing, diarrhea, abdominal cramping, or reactions to foods, heat, alcohol, or medications. These symptoms may prompt evaluation for mast cell activation disorders. Mast cell symptoms can worsen tachycardia and orthostatic intolerance through vessel dilation and inflammatory signaling.

#### Why subtype labels should stay flexible

Subtype labels work best as hypotheses. They help explain why one patient needs more vascular support while another needs heart rate control or volume expansion. They work poorly when used as fixed categories. Patients should expect clinicians to revise the working model as symptoms, test results, and treatment responses become clearer.

#### A patient-centered summary

POTS subtypes describe patterns within a complex syndrome. Neuropathic POTS emphasizes nerve control of blood vessels. Hyperadrenergic POTS emphasizes sympathetic activation. Hypovolemic POTS emphasizes low circulating volume. Secondary POTS emphasizes another associated condition. Many patients fit more than one pattern. The useful question is not “Which single subtype am I?” but “Which mechanisms matter most for my symptoms and treatment?”
