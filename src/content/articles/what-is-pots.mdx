## The basic definition

Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome<Cite ids="lauPosturalOrthostaticTachycardia2026" />, usually shortened to POTS, is a disorder of orthostatic intolerance. “Postural” means related to body position. “Orthostatic” means upright posture. “Tachycardia” means fast heart rate. “Syndrome” means a pattern of symptoms that often occur together. Put together, POTS describes a condition where standing causes an excessive rise in heart rate along with symptoms that improve when the person lies down.

POTS is not simply “being out of shape,” “being anxious,” or “having a fast pulse.” It is a problem with how the body regulates circulation and other automatic functions during position changes. A person with POTS may feel relatively stable while lying down, then develop dizziness, palpitations, shakiness, shortness of breath, nausea, brain fog, weakness, chest discomfort, or near-fainting after standing.

### What happens when a healthy person stands

When anyone stands, gravity pulls blood toward the legs and abdomen. The body normally corrects this quickly. Blood vessels tighten, heart rate rises slightly, and the brain continues receiving enough blood. Most people never notice the adjustment.

In POTS, this adjustment works poorly<Cite ids="rajDiagnosisManagementPostural2022" />. The heart rate rises more than expected because the body struggles to maintain stable blood flow while upright. Blood pressure does not need to drop for POTS to occur. This distinction matters because many people associate faintness only with low blood pressure. POTS can cause severe upright symptoms even when blood pressure readings look normal.

### What the heart rate pattern means

The common adult diagnostic threshold is a sustained heart rate increase of at least 30 beats per minute within 10 minutes of standing or head-up tilt. For adolescents, the threshold is often at least 40 beats per minute. Clinicians also look for chronic orthostatic symptoms and exclude other explanations, such as dehydration, anemia, medication effects, endocrine disorders, or prolonged bed rest.

#### Why the name sounds more specific than the illness feels

The name focuses on heart rate, but POTS affects more than the heart<Cite ids="steinbergNarrativeReviewPostural2023" />. The heart rate rise is measurable, which makes it useful for diagnosis. The daily experience often includes fatigue, temperature sensitivity, digestive symptoms, sleep disruption, headache, exercise intolerance, and trouble concentrating. Many patients describe POTS as a whole-body condition because the autonomic nervous system influences many organs at once.

#### Why symptoms change from day to day

POTS symptoms often fluctuate. Heat, dehydration, menstrual cycles, infections, poor sleep, large meals, alcohol, standing still, and overexertion can worsen symptoms. Lying down, fluids, salt when medically appropriate, compression garments, pacing, and structured conditioning often reduce symptom burden. Fluctuation does not mean the illness is imaginary. It reflects a control system that reacts strongly to context.

## POTS as dysautonomia

POTS belongs to a broader category called dysautonomia<Cite ids="steinbergNarrativeReviewPostural2023 rajDiagnosisManagementPostural2022" />. Dysautonomia means dysfunction of the autonomic nervous system, the system that regulates heart rate, blood pressure, sweating, digestion, temperature control, bladder function, and other automatic processes.

The autonomic nervous system does not operate like a simple on-off switch. It constantly adjusts signals to match posture, stress, temperature, hydration, digestion, and activity. In POTS, the standing response becomes unstable. The body compensates, but the compensation creates symptoms.

### Common symptoms

Symptoms vary by person, but many patients report lightheadedness, palpitations, tremulousness, weakness, fatigue, shortness of breath, chest discomfort, headache, nausea, abdominal pain, bloating, diarrhea or constipation, sweating changes, heat intolerance, cold hands and feet, sleep problems, and brain fog. Some people faint. Others rarely faint but feel close to fainting often.

### Who gets POTS

POTS often begins in adolescence or early adulthood, and it affects females more often than males. Some people develop symptoms after an infection, concussion, surgery, pregnancy, or a period of physical stress. Others develop symptoms gradually. POTS also overlaps with conditions such as migraine, hypermobility spectrum disorders, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, mast cell activation symptoms, autoimmune disease, and long COVID, though overlap does not mean every patient has these conditions.

#### Why POTS is often misunderstood

POTS can be difficult to recognize because symptoms cross medical specialties. Palpitations may lead to cardiology. Dizziness may lead to neurology. nausea may lead to gastroenterology. fatigue may lead to primary care or rheumatology. A patient may collect normal tests from several clinics while no one checks upright heart rate carefully.

The symptoms also resemble anxiety in some moments.<Cite ids="xuPediatricPosturalOrthostatic2024" /> A racing heart, shakiness, sweating, and breathlessness can look like panic. The key difference is that POTS symptoms often follow posture and improve with recumbence. Anxiety may coexist, but posture-linked tachycardia deserves evaluation on its own terms.

#### What POTS is not

POTS is not a structural heart disease in most patients. It is not the same as a heart rhythm disorder, although clinicians sometimes test for arrhythmias when symptoms require it. It is not diagnosed from a single high heart rate reading after stress, caffeine, fever, dehydration, or exercise. It is also not defined by fainting alone. Some people with POTS faint, but many do not.

#### Why diagnosis helps

Diagnosis gives patients a name for a pattern that often feels confusing. It also guides care. Treatment usually combines education, fluid and salt strategies when appropriate, compression, physical reconditioning, trigger management, and sometimes medications. The goal is not merely to lower heart rate. The goal is to improve upright tolerance, daily function, and quality of life.

#### A practical way to think about POTS

A useful placeholder explanation is this: POTS is a standing problem caused by abnormal autonomic and circulatory regulation. When the person stands, the body works too hard to keep blood moving effectively. The heart speeds up, symptoms appear, and lying down reduces the demand. The condition is real, measurable, and variable. It affects people differently, so care must match the person rather than the name alone.
