### The diagnostic question

POTS diagnosis starts with a practical question: does upright posture reliably trigger symptoms and an excessive heart rate increase, without another condition better explaining the pattern? The diagnosis is not based on one symptom, one device reading, or one abnormal day. Clinicians look for a consistent relationship between standing and symptoms.

A patient often arrives with a long list of complaints: dizziness, palpitations, fatigue, brain fog, nausea, tremor, exercise intolerance, heat sensitivity, and near-fainting. The clinician’s job is to connect those symptoms to posture, measure the cardiovascular response, and rule out common alternatives.

#### Core diagnostic criteria

Common criteria include a sustained heart rate increase of at least 30 beats per minute within 10 minutes of standing in adults, or at least 40 beats per minute in adolescents. The person must have frequent symptoms of orthostatic intolerance, usually for at least three months. The heart rate pattern must occur without significant orthostatic hypotension, commonly defined as a blood pressure drop of at least 20 mm Hg systolic or 10 mm Hg diastolic.

These thresholds guide diagnosis, but clinicians still use judgment. A person with fever, dehydration, anemia, hyperthyroidism, stimulant medication effects, or acute blood loss may show orthostatic tachycardia without having POTS. Diagnosis requires context.

#### Why symptoms matter as much as numbers

Heart rate alone does not diagnose POTS. Some people have a high standing heart rate because of deconditioning, illness, pain, anxiety, pregnancy, medication, or dehydration. POTS requires symptoms that fit orthostatic intolerance. The person feels worse upright and better lying down.

Symptoms also help clinicians estimate severity. A person who meets the heart rate threshold but functions normally needs a different approach than someone who cannot shower, shop, attend school, or work without symptoms.

##### Measuring heart rate and blood pressure

Clinicians usually measure heart rate and blood pressure after the patient lies quietly, then again after standing. Measurements are often taken at intervals over 10 minutes. The test may happen in an office, autonomic lab, or tilt-table setting.

Good measurement technique matters. The patient should rest before baseline readings. The room should not be excessively hot. The clinician should document medications, hydration, caffeine, recent illness, and time of day. These factors change results.

##### Active stand test

An active stand test uses the patient’s own standing effort. The patient lies down, rests, then stands while heart rate and blood pressure are monitored. This test resembles daily life because the leg muscles engage during standing. It is also easier to perform than tilt-table testing.

The active stand test can miss some cases or exaggerate others, depending on technique and patient state. It works best when clinicians combine the numbers with the symptom story.

### Tilt-table testing

A tilt-table test places the patient on a motorized table that moves from lying to upright. Straps keep the patient safe while monitors record heart rate and blood pressure. The test reduces leg-muscle assistance, which can make orthostatic stress more controlled.

Tilt-table testing is useful when the diagnosis remains unclear, when fainting is prominent, or when clinicians need to distinguish POTS from vasovagal syncope, orthostatic hypotension, or other autonomic disorders. Not every patient needs a tilt-table test. Many diagnoses use history, exam, and standing vitals.

#### Ruling out other causes

A responsible POTS evaluation asks what else explains tachycardia or orthostatic symptoms. Common checks include blood count for anemia, metabolic panel, thyroid testing, ferritin or iron studies when indicated, pregnancy testing when relevant, medication review, hydration status, and screening for infection or inflammatory disease when symptoms point that way.

Clinicians may also use electrocardiography, ambulatory rhythm monitoring, echocardiography, or exercise testing when symptoms suggest a primary cardiac problem. These tests do not diagnose POTS directly. They help exclude other conditions.

#### Conditions that can look similar

Several conditions overlap with POTS. Orthostatic hypotension causes symptoms after standing because blood pressure drops. Vasovagal syncope causes fainting through reflex changes in heart rate and blood pressure. Inappropriate sinus tachycardia causes a high heart rate that is less tightly linked to standing. Panic attacks can cause tachycardia, sweating, trembling, and breathlessness, but they do not usually create a reproducible 10-minute orthostatic heart rate pattern.

##### Medication and substance review

Medication review is essential. Stimulants, some antidepressants, diuretics, vasodilators, thyroid medication excess, bronchodilators, nicotine, alcohol, and high caffeine intake can affect heart rate or blood pressure. Stopping or changing medication requires medical guidance, but identifying these contributors prevents misdiagnosis.

##### Lab tests and their limits

Normal labs do not rule out POTS. Many patients have normal blood counts, electrolytes, thyroid function, and inflammatory markers. That does not make symptoms less real. It means routine testing did not find a different cause.

At the same time, abnormal labs can matter. Iron deficiency, anemia, dehydration, endocrine problems, and autoimmune disease can worsen orthostatic symptoms. Treating those problems may reduce symptoms even if POTS remains.

##### Wearables and home logs

Wearables can help patients notice patterns, but consumer devices do not replace medical diagnosis. Home logs are most useful when they record posture, symptoms, heart rate, blood pressure if available, hydration, meals, sleep, menstrual cycle, medications, and triggers. A short, structured log often helps more than hundreds of isolated screenshots.

##### What to bring to an appointment

Patients can bring a concise timeline: when symptoms began, what makes them better or worse, fainting history, current medications, major diagnoses, infections or injuries before onset, fluid and salt intake, functional limits, and examples of standing heart rate changes. Clear information shortens the path from suspicion to evaluation.

##### What a diagnosis changes

A POTS diagnosis shifts care from repeated reassurance to targeted management. It supports patient education, trigger reduction, fluid and salt planning when appropriate, compression, recumbent or semi-recumbent exercise progression, and medication decisions when symptoms remain limiting. It also gives clinicians a framework for related problems such as migraine, digestive symptoms, hypermobility, sleep disruption, and fatigue.
